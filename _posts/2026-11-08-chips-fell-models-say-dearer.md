<p class="lede">On 5 November the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.3%, its worst day since March 2020. In the same week I switched all 60 semiconductor models on my map to a standard DCF. The result: prices fell, but targets measured with the new method fell further. Three things this week: why the sell-off left these stocks dearer rather than cheaper, how far Google's TPU really trails NVIDIA, and why a cheaper new model tier raises revenue per gigawatt instead of lowering it.</p>

<div class="keypoints" markdown="1">
**This week in brief**

- Across 33 non-Taiwan semiconductor names, the median share price fell 18.8% and the median target fell 27.0%; for 25 of them the target fell more than the price.
- The cut comes mostly from the method, not the sell-off. For NVIDIA the DCF fell from US$235 to US$125, mainly because the discount rate rose from 10% to about 14%.
- Compared at the same precision, Google's TPU v7 costs about 1.06 times NVIDIA's VR200 per token, not the 2 times I had before.
- A new tier, GPT-6.1 Terra, is half the price and more productive; taking part of Sol's share, it lifts annual revenue per GW from US$49.5bn to US$51.8bn.
- [Tokenomics](https://yinchenchang.github.io/company-models/tokenomics/) is now live as interactive pages.
</div>

## 1. What happened on 5 November

Democrats won both chambers of Congress in the midterms, and investors worried that AI and data center construction would face more regulation. The SOX closed at 9,011, down 12.25%, its largest one-day fall since 16 March 2020; the iShares Semiconductor ETF lost about 23% in the week to 5 November.

My map's companies moved with it. From the 23 October to the 5 November close, the median share price of the 33 non-Taiwan semiconductor and IT-equipment names fell 18.8%. The largest falls were Marvell (−42%), SanDisk (−35%) and Lumentum (−33%).

## 2. Why a sell-off left them dearer

You would expect a falling price to mean more upside. Each dot below is one company: the horizontal axis is its price change, the vertical axis its target change. A company on the dashed line saw its target fall exactly as much as its price.

{% include charts/2026-11-08/scatter-en.svg %}
<p class="chart-note">33 non-Taiwan names. Targets from the AI Investment Map builds of 31 October and 8 November; Taiwan-listed targets are not shown on this site. Hover over a dot for its numbers.</p>

Twenty-five of the 33 sit below the line: their targets fell more than their prices. The median upside moved from −9.9% to −18.0%. Measured with the new ruler, the group is more expensive after the crash than two weeks earlier.

That is not a darker view of the businesses. I changed to a stricter ruler.

### The old ruler and the new one

- **Old (simplified):** earnings per share times a payout ratio, treated as cash to shareholders, discounted at a flat 10% for every company.
- **New (standard DCF):** cash generated by the business (after-tax operating profit plus depreciation, minus capital spending and the increase in working capital), discounted at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). The WACC depends on each stock's volatility relative to the market (beta), not a flat 10%.

Chip stocks have been very volatile for five years. Estimating beta myself from five years of monthly returns for 13 US chip peers gives NVIDIA a beta of about 1.9 and a WACC of about 14.4%. Raising the required return from 10% to 14% takes a large slice off the present value of distant cash flows.

{% include charts/2026-11-08/nvda-dcf-en.svg %}
<p class="chart-note">NVIDIA's DCF target from the old method to the new one, one change at a time. Blue: start and end. Orange: cuts. Green: additions.</p>

NVIDIA's DCF fell from US$235 to US$125, about US$110. The largest piece is the discount rate (−60), then the terminal value method (−31: long-run growth cut from 4% to 3%, with new investment assumed to earn only its cost of capital), then my own beta (−16). The definition of cash flow itself accounts for only US$6. **Almost all of the cut comes from how high a return I demand from these cash flows, not from a changed view of NVIDIA's business.**

### The other half follows the market down

My target is half DCF and half peer comparison. The peer half uses peers' price-to-earnings ratios, which fall when peers' prices fall. After 5 November the median forward P/E of NVIDIA's peers dropped from 18.7 to 16.9 times, and the comparison value from US$453 to US$409.

That is the built-in limit of relative valuation: when a whole industry falls together, it cannot tell you anything got cheaper, because the yardstick got cheaper too. Only the DCF half gives an independent answer in a crash.

Neoclouds work differently. My CoreWeave and Nebius models fund part of the build-out with new shares, issued at the current price: the lower the price, the more shares, the lower the value per share. So their targets fall with the price. CoreWeave's base target went from US$32.0 to US$25.5 and Nebius's from US$21.4 to US$10.3; both stay shorts.

## 4. Google's TPU versus NVIDIA

One question Tokenomics answers is what each chip costs per token produced. On 3 November (v21.9) I compared each chip at its own best native precision: VR200 at FP4, TPU v7 at FP8, its highest. That made the TPU twice as expensive per token as the VR200.

A check this week found the comparison unfair. Much of FP4's gain comes from saving memory bandwidth, and TPU v7 has about the same memory bandwidth per kW as VR200; TPU v7's true contemporary is also GB300, not VR200. Comparing both at FP8:

{% include charts/2026-11-08/tpu-en.svg %}
<p class="chart-note">Cost per token as a multiple of NVIDIA VR200 (VR200 = 1; higher is more expensive). The native-precision result is kept as the low case.</p>

TPU v7 drops from 2.0 to 1.06 times VR200, roughly level; Trainium3 from 4.4 to 2.2 times. For Alphabet, its in-house chip's cost gap is much smaller than I thought. For NVIDIA, its edge lies in new precisions such as FP4, and how much of that survives real traffic still needs measurement.

## 5. Why a cheaper tier raises revenue

OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Terra sits between the mid-tier Sol and the entry tier Luna, at about half Sol's price with performance close to Sol. I added it as Tokenomics' fourth tier, assuming 15% of 2026 paid tokens move from Sol to Terra.

{% include charts/2026-11-08/pq-flow-en.svg %}

Revenue per GW is price times quantity. Terra lowers the average price, but it is smaller and faster than Sol, so one GW produces more tokens, and quantity rises by more:

{% include charts/2026-11-08/terra-en.svg %}

The average effective price falls 6.0% and paid capacity per GW rises 4.8%; together revenue per GW rises 4.5%, from US$49.5bn to US$51.8bn a year. A cheaper product need not lower revenue; what matters is how much compute it saves.

The last row needs a warning. Monetisation m (realised price ÷ effective list price) rose 6.4%, but that is **not** better monetisation. Its denominator is the mix's effective list price; Terra made the denominator cheaper, so the ratio rose mechanically. When a ratio improves, first ask whether the numerator or the denominator moved.

## 6. A small fix from earnings season

None of the four hyperscalers' third-quarter filings depreciates IT equipment over four years, and Nebius moved to five years from January. The high-cost case in Tokenomics therefore goes from four to five years. The top of the holding-cost range falls; the base case is unchanged.

## What to watch

- **Sensitivity to the discount rate.** Most of the cut came from beta, estimated from 2021–2026 returns, the most volatile stretch of the AI rally. If volatility settles, WACC falls and targets rise. I will add "target per 0.3 lower beta" to the model pages.
- **Whether regulation reaches capital spending.** The market has moved first. The evidence will be 2027 capex guidance; Alphabet expects to give details on its fourth-quarter call.
- **Anthropic's listing.** The amended S-1 is public and the roadshow can start after 10 November; the price range will be the first market quote for a model lab.
